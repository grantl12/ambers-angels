Coordinator-Directed Drone Swarm: Remote Deployment for Missing Persons Search

Grant Lindberg, Founder & Executive Director — Amber's Angels Inc.

Abstract

Volunteer drone operators represent an untapped force multiplier for missing persons response, but the traditional model — drive to a staging area, brief with an incident commander, fly manual patterns — limits participation to people who can physically deploy. This paper describes a coordinator-directed drone swarm system that inverts the model: volunteers register their drones from home, the platform maintains a heartbeat-based presence map, and when an alert fires, a remote coordinator plans and dispatches autonomous waypoint missions to drones that may be miles from the search area. The drone executes the flight plan on its onboard flight controller — streaming live video for automated license plate recognition and vehicle classification or for human visual search. The result is aerial coverage within minutes of an alert, without requiring a single volunteer to leave their house.

The Relinquish-to-Swarm Model

The core idea is simple: a volunteer with a drone, kids at home, and nowhere to go can still contribute an aerial search. The platform handles everything between "I have a drone" and "there is a camera over the search area."

We call this relinquish to swarm. The pilot powers on their drone at home, opens the Amber's Angels app, and the drone begins reporting its GPS position to the platform every 30 seconds. From that point, the pilot has relinquished operational control to the coordinator network. Their drone appears on the mission map — an amber icon if the heartbeat is current, grayed out if the last heartbeat is older than five minutes. The pilot does not choose where to fly, does not plan a route, and does not need to be near the search area. They have offered an asset. The coordinator decides how to use it.

When a FEMA AMBER Alert, Silver Alert, or law enforcement BOLO fires, a coordinator on the platform can see every available drone, its home position, its battery level, and its VLOS radius. The coordinator selects a drone, picks a target — an observation point, a parking lot, a water body — and the platform generates a waypoint mission. The mission is pushed to the pilot's phone. The pilot taps Accept & Launch. The drone uploads the flight plan to its onboard flight controller and takes off autonomously.

The pilot's role at this point is legal presence: a Part 107 certificate holder standing near the takeoff point, monitoring battery and airspace. The coordinator's role is tactical: watching the live camera feed, adjusting the search area, dispatching additional drones if needed. The platform's role is execution: flight planning, waypoint generation, VLOS enforcement, mission lifecycle management, and timeout cleanup.

"Every volunteer who owns a drone is a potential search asset. The question is not whether they are willing to help — it is whether the system can turn their willingness into airborne coverage before the window closes."

Drone Registration and Heartbeat

Before a drone can participate in the swarm, the pilot registers it through the app. Registration records the drone model, serial number, camera horizontal field of view (default 84 degrees for DJI cameras), and maximum flight time. The platform creates a record in the autonomous_drones table linked to the pilot's account.

Once registered, the drone enters the presence pool via the heartbeat mechanism. The AutonomousMissionScreen in the mobile app sends a POST /autonomous/drones/{id}/heartbeat request every 30 seconds. The heartbeat payload contains the drone's current GPS position — preferring the DJI SDK's aircraft GPS when a drone is connected, falling back to the phone's device GPS via Expo Location otherwise. The server updates the drone's home_lat, home_lng, and last_seen_at timestamp on each heartbeat.

Coordinators see the drone pool on their mission map. A drone with a heartbeat within the last five minutes is considered online. Beyond five minutes, it grays out — the pilot may have closed the app, lost connectivity, or powered down the aircraft. Only online drones are dispatchable. The ownership model is strict: only the pilot registered to a drone (or an admin) can send heartbeats for it, preventing spoofed positions.

Mission Planning

The platform supports three mission types, each optimized for a different search scenario.

Observation Post

The primary dispatch mode. The coordinator selects a target location — either by tapping the map to place an explicit latitude/longitude or by supplying a polygon whose centroid is used. The drone flies to the observation point at a configurable altitude (default 60 m AGL) and speed (default 8 m/s), then hovers. While hovering, the drone's live RTMP stream is processed by the server-side detection pipeline: OpenALPR for license plate recognition and YOLOv8-nano for vehicle classification (color, body type, make/model). The observation post is a single-waypoint mission — the simplest possible flight plan, designed for intersection coverage or entry/exit monitoring.

Endpoint: POST /autonomous/plan. The request accepts obs_lat/obs_lng (preferred) or polygon_geojson (centroid extracted). Returns a mission with status pending and a single waypoint.

Sweep (Lawnmower Pattern)

For area coverage — parking lots, neighborhoods, commercial zones where a target vehicle might be parked. The coordinator draws a polygon on the map. The platform generates a boustrophedon (lawnmower) waypoint path that covers the polygon interior with overlapping scan lanes.

Lane spacing is computed from the camera's horizontal field of view, the mission altitude, and a configurable overlap percentage (default 30%). At the default sweep altitude of 15 m AGL with an 84-degree HFOV, each lane covers approximately 28 m of ground width with 8.4 m of overlap between adjacent lanes. The sweep angle is optimized across four candidates (0, 45, 90, and 135 degrees) to minimize the number of lanes — the angle whose rotated bounding box produces the smallest perpendicular extent wins.

Speed is set to 3 m/s by default for plate readability. The lower altitude and slower speed compared to observation post missions reflect the different objective: the sweep is looking for a stationary or slow-moving vehicle, not monitoring traffic flow.

Endpoint: POST /autonomous/plan-sweep. Requires polygon_geojson. Returns waypoint count and full waypoint list.

Water Body Perimeter Search

For Purple Alert and other scenarios where the missing person may be near water. Described in detail in AA-2026-001.1 The coordinator selects a water body from a ranked list (queried from OpenStreetMap Overpass API, cached with 24-hour TTL). The platform generates a closed-loop perimeter flight plan at a configurable buffer distance from shore (default 20 m for tree clearance) and altitude (default 15 m AGL).

The perimeter path is computed by offsetting the water body polygon outward using edge-normal projection, then simplifying with Douglas-Peucker to fit within the DJI MSDK V5 waypoint limit of 98 per mission. Waypoints include computed headings so the drone's camera tracks along the shoreline. Speed is set to 3 m/s for visual search fidelity. This is a human visual search — the coordinator watches the live feed and the ALPR/YOLO pipeline is not the primary sensor.

Endpoint: POST /autonomous/plan-water-search. Requires water_body_id and polygon_geojson.

Waypoint Generation and KMZ Packaging

All waypoint generation happens server-side in Python. The output is a list of waypoint dictionaries, each containing latitude, longitude, altitude (meters AGL), heading (degrees, 0 = north), and speed (m/s). Coordinates are rounded to 8 decimal places — sub-millimeter precision that exceeds GPS accuracy but avoids floating-point truncation artifacts.

The coordinate math uses a local metric projection centered on the polygon centroid: latitude differences scale at 111,111 m/degree, longitude differences scale at 111,111 × cos(latitude) m/degree. This avoids the overhead of a full UTM projection while remaining accurate within the operational radius of a single drone mission (typically under 2 km).

On the mobile side, waypoints are serialized to JSON and passed across the React Native bridge to the DJI MSDK V5 native module. The Kotlin implementation (DJICameraImpl.kt) builds a WPML XML document conforming to DJI's Waypoint Mission Markup Language specification (namespace http://www.dji.com/wpmz/1.0.2). The XML is written into a KMZ archive (a ZIP file containing wpmz/waylines.wpml) and pushed to the aircraft via the WaypointMissionManager.

Dynamic Aircraft Identification

The WPML droneEnumValue field must match the connected aircraft model. The implementation reads the ProductType from the DJI SDK at runtime and maps it to the correct enum value — 91 for Mini 4 Pro, 89 for Mini 3/Mini 3 Pro/M350 RTK, 77 for Mavic 3, 67 for M30 series, 60 for M300 RTK and Mavic 3 Enterprise. This dynamic mapping was added after testing revealed that a hardcoded enum value causes mission upload failure on mismatched aircraft.

Mission Lifecycle

Every mission follows a defined state machine. Status transitions are timestamp-annotated and persisted in the autonomous_missions table.

Mission Status Lifecycle
StepAction
01pending. Mission created by coordinator. Waypoints generated and stored. Mission appears in the pilot's pending list on the Autonomous Mission Screen. Pilot polls for pending missions every 30 seconds.
02uploading. Pilot taps "Accept & Launch." The app sends a pre-mission acknowledgment (including FAA waiver number for BVLOS missions), then calls startWaypointMission() which builds the KMZ and pushes it to the aircraft via WaypointMissionManager.pushKMZFileToAircraft(). Upload progress events fire to the UI.
03executing. KMZ uploaded successfully. WaypointMissionManager.startMission() called. The flight controller begins autonomous waypoint execution. The started_at timestamp is set. Both executing and active are accepted status strings — the mobile DJI SDK emits executing while the backend treats both identically.
04relook. (Sweep/observation missions only.) The detection pipeline identified a medium-confidence plate match on the drone's RTMP stream. The mission is paused for sharper frame capture. See Auto-Relook section below.
05ready_for_control. (Water search missions only.) The drone has completed its perimeter trace and hovers at the final waypoint. The coordinator retains live camera view for continued visual search. The pilot sees "Perimeter complete — hovering" and can tap "Complete Mission" when the coordinator is satisfied.
06completed. Mission finished successfully. completed_at timestamp set. The drone returns home (observation/sweep) or remains hovering (water search, until explicit completion).
07aborted. Pilot or coordinator manually cancelled the mission. The app calls stopWaypointMission() which halts the flight controller — the drone hovers in place. RTH can be triggered separately.
08failed. Mission timed out or encountered an unrecoverable error. Error message stored in error_msg field.

Timeout Cleanup

A background task (mission_timeout_loop) runs every five minutes and expires stale missions:

Stuck StatusTimeoutError Message
pending / dispatched / uploading30 minutes"Timed out waiting for drone to connect"
executing / active / ready_for_control4 hours"Mission exceeded maximum flight duration"

These timeouts catch real failure modes: a pilot who accepted a mission but never connected their drone (30-minute timeout), or a drone that lost telemetry mid-flight and the server never received a completion status (4-hour timeout). Expired missions are set to failed with completed_at = NOW() and logged with their IDs for debugging.

Auto-Relook: Handling Ambiguous Detections

The detection pipeline produces a confidence score for every license plate read. Scores above the HIGH_CONFIDENCE_EVENT_MIN_SCORE threshold (85%) trigger an immediate alert — Discord notification, push notification, and optionally SMS. But what about a read at 78%? The plate might match. The camera angle was bad, or the plate was partially occluded, or the lighting washed out a character. A human would reposition for a better look. The auto-relook flow automates that repositioning.

Auto-Relook Flow
StepAction
01Medium-confidence detection. The unified worker detects a plate match between the single-frame floor (55%) and the high-confidence threshold (85%) on an active drone's RTMP stream.
02Relook request. The worker calls POST /autonomous/missions/{id}/relook with the plate text and confidence score. The backend transitions the mission to relook status — only if the mission is currently executing or active.
03Drone pauses. A push notification is sent to the pilot's phone via Expo. The app calls pauseMission() on the DJI SDK. The drone holds its current position in a stable hover.
04Sharper frames. Stationary hover produces significantly sharper video frames than in-flight capture. The worker evaluates 3–5 additional frames from the now-stable RTMP stream.
05Confirmation or resume. If the re-evaluation exceeds the high-confidence threshold, the alert fires normally. POST /autonomous/missions/{id}/relook-complete?confirmed=true is called. If the frames do not confirm, confirmed=false is sent. In both cases, the mission status transitions back to executing and the drone resumes its waypoint path automatically.

The relook mechanism eliminates a class of false negatives that would otherwise be lost. Without it, a medium-confidence read from a moving drone is discarded and the drone continues past the target. With it, the system pauses, looks again, and either confirms the match or resumes the search — no human intervention required.

FAA Compliance: Operational Tiers

The platform enforces FAA regulatory constraints at mission plan creation time, not as a post-flight audit. Three operational tiers are defined, each with progressively looser constraints and correspondingly higher authorization requirements.

VLOS Radius Enforcement

For VLOS missions, the platform computes the haversine distance from the drone's home position (last heartbeat GPS) to every generated waypoint. If any waypoint exceeds the VLOS radius, the mission is rejected with a clear error message showing the distance and the limit. The coordinator is advised to either move the observation point closer or switch to bvlos_tactical if a waiver is on file.

The default 400 m radius is conservative — Part 107 does not specify a numerical VLOS distance, only that the remote PIC must maintain unaided visual contact. In practice, a Mini 4 Pro is difficult to see beyond 400 m. The radius is configurable per drone (50–10,000 m range) via PATCH /autonomous/drones/{id}, allowing administrators to set appropriate limits based on the aircraft size and local conditions.

Pre-Mission Acknowledgment

Before any mission launches, the pilot must confirm through an in-app acknowledgment modal that they will observe only — they will not pursue, confront, or approach any person or vehicle. This is not a legal waiver; it is a behavioral reminder. For BVLOS missions, the modal additionally requires the pilot to enter their FAA certificate or waiver number, which is stored with the mission record in the bvlos_certificate column.

Safety Architecture

Consumer drones have mature safety systems. The platform's job is not to replicate them but to configure them correctly and get out of the way. Safety is handled at three layers: the flight controller firmware, the WPML configuration, and the platform's lifecycle management.

Flight Controller (Firmware Level)

Return to Home on RC loss. The WPML configuration sets exitOnRCLost=executeLostAction and executeRCLostAction=goBack. If the remote controller loses signal, the drone ascends to RTH altitude (mission altitude + 20 m) and flies home autonomously. This is firmware behavior — it works regardless of app connectivity.

Battery RTH. DJI firmware calculates the power required to return home and triggers automatic RTH when the remaining charge is insufficient. This is independent of the platform — the drone will abandon a mission to save itself.

Obstacle avoidance. Aircraft with vision sensors (Mini 4 Pro, Mavic 3, M30 series) operate in BRAKE mode during autonomous missions. If the obstacle avoidance system detects a collision risk, the drone brakes and hovers rather than diverting around the obstacle. This prevents unpredictable deviations from the planned flight path.

GEO zones. DJI's geofencing system prevents flight in restricted airspace (airports, prisons, stadiums, etc.). This is enforced at the firmware level and cannot be overridden by the platform.

KMZ Execution Model

Once the KMZ waypoint file is uploaded to the aircraft, the flight controller executes the mission independently. If the pilot's phone disconnects, crashes, or loses cellular signal, the drone continues its waypoint path. This is by design: the mission lives on the flight controller, not on the phone. The phone is a status display, not a control link. On mission completion, the drone returns home (observation/sweep) or hovers (water search) per the finishAction parameter in the WPML.

Platform Level

Timeout cleanup. Missions that stall in pre-flight statuses for 30 minutes or in active flight for 4 hours are automatically failed. This prevents zombie missions from accumulating in the pending queue.

Abort. The pilot can tap Abort at any time. The app calls stopWaypointMission() via the DJI SDK, which halts the flight controller. The drone hovers in place. The pilot can then trigger Return to Home separately.

Pause and resume. Available during execution. The drone holds position on pause and continues from the exact pause point on resume. This enables the coordinator to inspect an area more closely via the live feed without losing the mission progress.

Notable exclusions. The DJI Air 3 is not supported — DJI has confirmed it has no MSDK V5 access. Operational Scenario

Consider a realistic deployment. An AMBER Alert fires at 2:14 PM on a Tuesday in Carrollton, Georgia. A white 2019 Honda Civic, plate YVJ024, last seen heading west on Highway 166. The FEMA IPAWS CMAS alert is ingested by the platform within 5 minutes. Vehicle targets and watchlist entries are created. Discord fires. Push notifications go to every volunteer in the Carrollton coverage area.

T+0:00 FEMA IPAWS ingestion. Alert processed. Plate "YVJ024" added to watchlist. Vehicle target created: white Honda Civic. Push notifications fire.
T+0:30 Coordinator dispatches. Three drones are online in the heartbeat pool. Coordinator selects Drone 1 (Mini 4 Pro, 1.2 km from Highway 166 interchange) and places an observation post over the westbound on-ramp. VLOS check passes at 380 m from home.
T+1:00 Sweep dispatched. Coordinator draws a polygon over the Walmart parking lot off Highway 166. Drone 2 (Mavic 3, 800 m away) receives a sweep mission. 24 waypoints generated. Lawnmower pattern at 15 m AGL, 3 m/s.
T+1:30 Pilot 1 accepts. Taps "Accept & Launch." Pre-mission acknowledgment confirmed. KMZ uploads to Mini 4 Pro. Drone lifts off and translates to observation point at 60 m AGL.
T+2:00 Both drones airborne. RTMP streams active. ALPR + YOLO processing every frame. The coordinator monitors the interchange feed. The worker scans the parking lot sweep.
T+4:30 Medium-confidence match. Sweep drone reads "YVJ024" at 72% confidence on a white sedan in the parking lot. Auto-relook triggered. Drone pauses. Hover frames evaluated.
T+5:00 Confirmed. Three stable hover frames read "YVJ024" at 94%, 91%, 96%. HIGH_CONFIDENCE alert fires. Discord embed with golden frame, GPS coordinates, confidence scores. Relook completes. Sweep resumes.
T+5:30 Coordinates relayed. Coordinator passes GPS pin to responding officers. Total time from FEMA ingestion to eyes on target vehicle: under 6 minutes.

Neither pilot left their house. Neither pilot chose where to fly. The coordinator directed two drones to two locations simultaneously from a phone screen. The detection pipeline identified the vehicle. The platform handled the flight planning, the waypoint generation, the VLOS enforcement, the auto-relook pause, and the alert dispatch. The volunteers provided the hardware and the legal presence. The platform provided everything else.

Limitations and Future Work

Single-drone missions. Each mission dispatches to one drone. Multi-drone coordination — searching multiple water bodies simultaneously, or sweeping adjacent parking lots in parallel — is currently manual (the coordinator dispatches multiple missions individually). Automated multi-drone task allocation is an engineering extension, not a design challenge: the heartbeat pool already tracks multiple drones, and the mission system already supports concurrent missions.

Night operations. Consumer drone cameras perform poorly in low light. Thermal payloads (available on M30T, Mavic 3 Enterprise) would significantly extend operational hours. The waypoint generation system is payload-agnostic — the same flight plans work with visual, thermal, or multispectral cameras. Only the detection pipeline needs to adapt.

iOS DJI SDK. The current DJI MSDK V5 native module is Kotlin (Android only). iOS DJI SDK requires a separate Swift implementation and a macOS build machine. The mobile app includes a Platform.OS !== 'android' guard that falls back to phone camera mode on iOS. This is a build-environment constraint, not an architectural one.

Concurrent relook handling. The current relook implementation handles one relook per mission at a time. If a second medium-confidence detection occurs while a relook is in progress, the second detection is not queued. This is acceptable for current operational scale but would need a relook queue for high-density sweep missions.

Airspace deconfliction. The mission map displays ADS-B aircraft positions (fetched via the OpenSky/ADS-B Exchange API) with closest-point-of-approach conflict detection (500 ft horizontal, 300 ft vertical within a 2-minute lookahead). This is advisory only — it does not prevent mission dispatch or trigger automatic avoidance maneuvers. Full LAANC integration for automated airspace authorization is a future capability.

Evidence chain of custody. The platform stores golden frames (high-confidence detection images) and GPS tracks but does not yet produce a formal evidence package (PDF with timeline, frames, chain-of-custody block). This is required for law enforcement handoff and is a near-term build priority.

Conclusion

The fundamental constraint in volunteer drone search is not hardware. Consumer drones are capable, affordable, and widely owned. The constraint is coordination: getting the right drone to the right location at the right time, with legal compliance, mission planning, and detection processing handled transparently. A volunteer who owns a Mini 4 Pro and a Part 107 certificate has everything needed to be a search asset — except the system that tells them where to point it.

The relinquish-to-swarm model solves this by separating the volunteer's contribution (hardware and legal presence) from the operational decisions (where to fly, what pattern, at what altitude). The coordinator makes the tactical call. The platform makes it executable. The drone makes it airborne. The detection pipeline makes it useful.

When the next AMBER Alert fires, the question should not be "can we get a drone in the air?" It should be "which three drones do we deploy first?"
