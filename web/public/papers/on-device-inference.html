On-Device Inference: Privacy by Architecture, Not Policy

Grant Lindberg, Founder & Executive Director — Amber’s Angels Inc.

Update — July 22, 2026

Since this paper was published, the architecture changed: on June 28, 2026, the background/foreground scan path on both platforms was given the ability to upload one JPEG frame as evidence when a scan produces a confirmed watchlist hit — through the exact native-code-and-app-review path this paper describes as the only way to break the guarantee (see “What Would It Take to Break This Guarantee?” below, which turned out to be a roadmap rather than a hypothetical). The guarantee this paper still holds: non-matching frames never leave the phone. The absolute “zero frames, ever” claims below are historical and no longer accurate; sections that changed are annotated inline. See the current Privacy Policy for the up-to-date claim.

Abstract

Amber’s Angels asks private citizens to mount their phones on dashboards and scan license plates during active missing persons alerts. That is an extraordinary ask. For it to work, volunteers must trust — and be able to verify — that their phones are not uploading video of every car they pass. This paper describes the architectural separation between the app’s two camera paths: a background passive scan that runs optical character recognition entirely on the phone and transmits only plate text, and an active pilot camera mode that intentionally uploads raw frames for evidence. The privacy guarantee of the background path is enforced by code structure, not by policy. There is no server endpoint that accepts frames from the background scanner, no configuration toggle that enables frame upload, and no over-the-air update that can change this without shipping a new native binary through app store review. When the architecture makes the wrong thing impossible, the policy becomes redundant.

The Two Paths

The Amber’s Angels mobile app contains two fundamentally different camera modes. They look similar to the user — both involve a phone camera pointed at license plates — but they have completely different privacy properties, different authentication requirements, and different data flows. Conflating them is the single most common misunderstanding about the platform, and the one most likely to kill volunteer recruitment.

Path 1: Background Passive Scan

The default volunteer experience. A volunteer mounts their phone on the dashboard, taps Start Mission, and drives. The phone captures a 640×480 JPEG from the rear camera at a configurable interval (default 1.5 seconds), passes each frame to Google ML Kit’s text recognition engine running on the phone’s own processor, extracts any text that matches the heuristic signature of a U.S. license plate (4–8 alphanumeric characters, at least one letter, at least one digit, not a common road word), and — if a candidate plate is found — sends a minimal payload to the server:

  • plate_text — the recognized characters (e.g., ABC1234)
  • plate_confidence — ML Kit’s confidence score
  • lat, lng — GPS coordinates
  • drone_id — a volunteer-chosen device identifier

That is it — for this endpoint. The raw camera capture is used as input to the on-device OCR model, and then it is discarded; the JPEG never touches a network socket at this step. POST /ingest/detection does not accept a file upload field — there is no file parameter in its API contract, and that remains true today. (Update, July 2026: on a confirmed watchlist hit, the phone makes a separate, second call to POST /ingest/frame to upload one evidence frame — see the note at the top of this paper. That call did not exist when this section was written.)

On Android, this runs as a foreground service with its own persistent notification. The volunteer can switch to Uber, Google Maps, or any other app — scanning continues in the background. The user sees a running count of plates read, and if a plate matches the active watchlist, the phone fires a high-priority notification with vibration. The entire pipeline from camera capture to OCR to plate candidate filtering to server POST runs in Kotlin native code, independent of the React Native UI layer.

Path 2: Active Pilot Camera

An explicit, opt-in action for pilots on active search missions. The volunteer taps Start Mission, the app confirms there is at least one active FEMA alert, and the camera begins posting raw JPEG frames directly to POST /ingest/frame at the configured interval. The server receives the full image, runs OpenALPR, applies CLAHE image enhancement if the first pass finds nothing, runs perspective deskew, classifies vehicles with YOLOv8, and feeds results into the aggregation pipeline. High-confidence frames are persisted as “golden frames” — evidence that can be included in law enforcement handoff packages.

This path requires a pilot JWT. Unauthenticated requests are rejected with HTTP 401. Frame size is capped at 25 MB. Only JPEG, PNG, and WebP content types are accepted. The pilot chose to be here — they tapped a button, the app checked for an active alert, and the camera activated. This is not passive scanning; it is evidence collection during an active search, and frame upload is both expected and required for the chain of custody to hold.

“The question is not whether we have a privacy policy. Every company has a privacy policy. The question is whether the architecture makes it physically impossible to violate the policy without shipping a new app binary.”

Why Architecture Beats Policy

Every surveillance technology company has a privacy policy. Flock Safety has one. Motorola Solutions has one. Axon has one. The policies say reassuring things: data is encrypted, access is role-based, retention periods are limited, images are not shared with unauthorized parties.

Then the policies change.

In 2023, Flock Safety updated its law enforcement contracts to allow longer data retention periods — from 30 days to 120 days and beyond — while the cameras on the poles continued capturing every vehicle that passed.1 The technology never changed. The cameras captured exactly the same data before and after the policy update. Only the rules about what happened to that data after capture changed. A policy-based privacy guarantee means the guarantee lasts exactly as long as the current board of directors wants it to last.

Amber’s Angels cannot afford that model. We are asking private citizens — not law enforcement officers, not paid contractors, private volunteers — to turn their personal phones into license plate readers. The difference between “community search tool” and “mass surveillance network” is not branding. It is whether the phone in the volunteer’s dashboard cradle is uploading video of every car in their neighborhood.

An architectural guarantee is different in kind from a policy guarantee. It is not a promise about what we choose to do with data. It is a statement about what the system is physically capable of doing.

What Would It Take to Break This Guarantee?

To add frame upload to the background scan path, a developer would need to:

  • Modify ScanService.kt to transmit the JPEG byte array instead of (or in addition to) the OCR result
  • Either add a file upload field to POST /ingest/detection on the server, or redirect the scan service to call POST /ingest/frame instead
  • Compile a new Android native binary containing the modified Kotlin code
  • Submit that binary to Google Play review (and Apple App Store review, once the iOS implementation exists)
  • Wait for store approval and distribution

This is not a toggle. It is not a server-side configuration change. It is not an over-the-air JavaScript update. The scan service is native Kotlin compiled into the Android APK. Changing its behavior requires a full native build, store submission, and review. That process takes days and produces a reviewable, diffable, auditable artifact.

Compare this to a policy change, which requires an email to a legal department and an updated PDF on a website.

(Update, July 2026: this is exactly what happened. The scan service was modified to call POST /ingest/frame on a watchlist hit, compiled into a new native build, and shipped through app store review — the reviewable, diffable process this section predicted. The point stands: it took a shipped app update, not a silent server-side or policy change.)

Technical Implementation

The On-Device OCR Pipeline

The background scan service (ScanService.kt) is an Android foreground service using the Camera2 API. It opens the rear-facing camera, configures an ImageReader at 640×480 resolution with JPEG output, and captures a still frame on a repeating interval.

When a frame arrives, the service decodes the JPEG into a Bitmap, wraps it in an ML Kit InputImage, and passes it to Google’s on-device text recognizer. ML Kit processes the image entirely on the phone’s CPU/GPU — no network call is made, no data leaves the device during recognition. The recognizer returns a VisionText object containing every text block it found in the image, each with a bounding box and a confidence score.

The service then applies a plate candidate filter. Not every text string in a camera frame is a license plate. Road signs say STOP and EXIT. Bumper stickers say FORD and HONDA. The filter enforces several heuristics:

  • Length between 4 and 8 characters (after stripping non-alphanumeric characters)
  • Must contain at least one digit (rejects pure-alpha strings like bumper text)
  • Pure-alpha strings longer than 4 characters are rejected (catches brand names)
  • Explicitly blocked common words: STOP, SLOW, EXIT, LANE, ONLY, TURN, KEEP, RIGHT, LEFT, FORD, JEEP, HONDA, TOYOTA, CHEVY, DODGE
  • ML Kit confidence must be at least 55%

If any candidates survive the filter, the highest-confidence match is posted to POST /ingest/detection as form data — not multipart, not a file upload, just key-value pairs. The server normalizes the plate text, checks it against the active watchlist, feeds it into the aggregation pipeline, and returns a watchlist_hit boolean. If the plate matches, the phone fires a heads-up notification.

The Server-Side Endpoint

POST /ingest/detection accepts the following form fields:

FieldTypeNotes
drone_idstring, requiredVolunteer-chosen device ID
plate_textstring, requiredRecognized characters
plate_confidencefloat, default 0.70ML Kit confidence (0–1 scale)
lat, lngfloat, optionalGPS coordinates
altitude, heading, speedfloat, optionalGPS metadata
pilot_idstring, optionalAuthenticated pilot username
sourcestring, default phone_mlkitDetection source tag

There is no file field. No image field. No frame field. The endpoint uses FastAPI’s Form(...) parameter type, not UploadFile. Even at the HTTP protocol level, it is not a multipart upload — it is a standard URL-encoded form POST. The server is architecturally incapable of receiving an image through this endpoint.

Authentication is also different. POST /ingest/detection uses an _optional_pilot dependency — it accepts a JWT if one is present but does not require it. This allows the Android background scan service to operate even when the user has not logged in. By contrast, POST /ingest/frame requires a valid pilot JWT via the get_current_pilot dependency. Unauthenticated frame uploads are rejected.

The frame_id field in the aggregation pipeline is set to None for the initial /ingest/detection POST — no image accompanies that call, full stop. (Update, July 2026: on a confirmed watchlist hit, the phone follows up with a call to POST /ingest/frame, a UUID is generated for that one frame, and it is saved to the golden frame directory. The Discord notification for a phone-scan hit now includes that evidence frame, the same as a drone hit. Non-hit detections still produce no frame and no Discord attachment — that part of the design is unchanged.)

The separation is total. The two paths share the same aggregation pipeline on the server — both feed into the same AggregationService with the same 5-second correlation window, the same watchlist lookup, the same confidence thresholds — but they arrive through different endpoints, with different authentication, different payloads, and fundamentally different privacy properties.

The Volunteer Trust Problem

I have had the same conversation dozens of times since we started recruiting volunteers in Carrollton, Georgia. It goes like this:

“So you want me to put an app on my phone that reads license plates? Who sees the video?”

The answer has to be nobody, and the person asking has to believe it. Not because I said so. Not because the privacy policy says so. Because the app is built in a way that makes it true regardless of what any future executive, board member, or acquirer might want.

This is not a theoretical concern. The history of surveillance technology is a history of mission creep. Red-light cameras were deployed to reduce intersection fatalities; cities started using them as revenue generators.2 License plate reader networks were built for stolen vehicle recovery; police departments started querying them for immigration enforcement.3 Ring doorbells were sold as home security devices; Amazon gave footage access to police departments without warrants.4

In every case, the technology was the same before and after the mission expanded. Only the policy changed. The hardware and software were always capable of the expanded use — someone just decided to use the capability.

Amber’s Angels cannot be that. If a volunteer’s neighbor sees the app running and asks “is your phone recording me?”, the answer needs to be verifiable. The phone is reading license plates and sending text — a string of characters — unless that specific plate matches an active alert, in which case one photo of that one vehicle is uploaded as evidence. It is not recording video, and it is not photographing anyone else's car. No server-side change can make it upload more than that, because the code that decides what to transmit runs on the phone itself, compiled into the app binary, subject to app store review.

“The question every volunteer asks is not ‘what does your privacy policy say?’ It is ‘is my phone recording my neighbors?’ Architecture lets me answer that question with a fact, not a promise.”

The Flock Comparison

Flock Safety, the dominant ALPR provider in the U.S. law enforcement market, deploys fixed cameras on poles that capture an image of every vehicle that passes. Those images — including the full vehicle, surrounding context, and sometimes occupants — are stored on Flock’s cloud servers. Access policies govern who can query the data, for how long it is retained, and what purposes are permissible.

For the overwhelming majority of scans — every plate that does not match an active alert — Amber’s Angels’ background scan path produces no image at all: no context to capture, no occupant to photograph. The data that reaches the server is ABC1234, 0.87, 33.5795, -85.0766. That is a plate number, a confidence score, and a GPS point. It is less data than a toll booth transponder generates. The one exception is a confirmed watchlist match, where a single evidence frame is uploaded — the same evidentiary standard applied to a drone hit, and disclosed in the Privacy Policy.

This is not a criticism of Flock’s engineering. Fixed-camera ALPR requires image capture — the camera is unattended, and human review of captured images is part of the evidence workflow. The architectures serve different operational models. But the privacy properties are categorically different, and that distinction matters when the operator is a private citizen driving their own car through their own neighborhood.

What Happens on a Match

When the background scanner reads a plate that appears on the active watchlist, the following occurs:

On-Device Watchlist Hit Flow
StepAction
01On-device OCR. ML Kit recognizes plate text from the camera frame. The frame is processed locally and never stored or transmitted.
02Plate candidate filter. The recognized text passes through the plate heuristic filter (length, character mix, common-word blocklist). Non-plate text is discarded.
03Server POST. The plate text, confidence, and GPS coordinates are sent to POST /ingest/detection as form-encoded key-value pairs. No image data.
04Watchlist lookup. The server normalizes the plate and checks it against the active watchlist. If the confidence exceeds 55% (after normalization to a 0–100 scale), the check proceeds.
05Aggregation. The detection enters the same 5-second aggregation window used by all detection sources. Multiple readings of the same plate within 5 seconds are correlated into a single snapshot with a composite confidence score.
06Evidence upload (hit only). (Added June 28, 2026.) The phone follows up with a call to POST /ingest/frame, uploading the one frame that produced the match. The server assigns it a UUID and saves it to the golden frame directory.
07Alert dispatch. If the aggregated confidence exceeds the high-confidence threshold (85%), a Discord alert fires with the plate, confidence, GPS, detection source, and the evidence frame from step 06 attached — the same as a drone hit.
08Volunteer notification. The phone receives the watchlist_hit: true response and fires a high-priority local notification with vibration, alerting the volunteer that they have driven past a vehicle matching an active alert.

A non-matching scan produces a detection event record with frame_id = None and frame_url = None — no evidence image, just a plate reading, a location, a timestamp, and a confidence score. That's the overwhelming majority of scans, and it's sufficient to direct law enforcement to the area without being sufficient for a positive identification or an arrest — nor should it be; the volunteer is a tipster, not a forensic analyst. A confirmed watchlist match is the one case that gets an evidence frame, per step 06 above.

The Active Camera Path: When Frames Are Needed

The active pilot camera mode exists because some operational scenarios require raw imagery. When a coordinator dispatches a drone to an observation point, or when a pilot is on an active search mission with a visual confirmation objective, the system needs high-resolution frames that can be run through server-side ALPR with CLAHE enhancement, perspective correction, and vehicle classification. These frames become part of the evidence chain — they are persisted as golden frames and can be included in law enforcement handoff packages.

This is an explicit, informed action. The pilot:

  • Has registered an account and authenticated with a JWT
  • Has navigated to the Camera screen and tapped Start Mission
  • Has been shown that at least one active FEMA alert exists (the camera gate)
  • Sees a live HUD showing “LIVE” status, frame count, and capture interval
  • Can stop at any time by tapping Stop Mission

The frame upload path runs server-side ALPR (OpenALPR), applies adaptive histogram equalization (CLAHE) for low-contrast frames, performs perspective deskew to correct for camera angle, and classifies the vehicle with YOLOv8-nano (color, body type, make/model). If a plate match exceeds the high-confidence threshold, the best frame is saved to the golden frame directory and a Discord alert fires with a frame thumbnail — because in this path, the frame exists and is evidence.

The privacy tradeoff is explicit and understood. The volunteer is not casually driving through their neighborhood. They are on a mission, in a mode they deliberately entered, uploading frames to a server operated by the nonprofit they volunteered for, in service of an active government-issued missing persons alert. Frame upload is the feature, not a side effect.

Limitations and Future Work

iOS On-Device Scanning

On-device OCR is implemented on both platforms. Android uses ML Kit inside a foreground service (ScanService.kt), allowing scanning to continue when the user switches to another app — a critical capability for gig economy drivers who need navigation or ride-share apps running simultaneously. iOS uses Apple’s Vision framework (VNRecognizeTextRequest) via a Swift native module, running in the foreground camera loop. iOS does not support background camera access, so volunteers must keep the app open while scanning.

Both platforms transmit plate text, confidence, and GPS to the server via POST /ingest/detection on every scan. Non-matching frames never leave the device on either platform; on a confirmed watchlist hit, both platforms follow up with the same one-frame upload to POST /ingest/frame. The privacy guarantee is identical across iOS and Android — the only difference is the background execution model.

On-Device Model Improvements

ML Kit’s general-purpose text recognizer is not specialized for license plates. It handles well-lit, well-framed plates adequately, but struggles with motion blur, low contrast, partial occlusion, and non-standard plate formats. A purpose-built on-device plate recognition model — smaller, faster, and tuned for the specific visual characteristics of U.S. license plates — would improve both accuracy and battery efficiency.

The constraint is training data. Building a plate-specific model requires a large corpus of plate images captured under real driving conditions, with ground truth labels. Amber’s Angels is accumulating this data through the drone RTMP path (where frames are processed server-side with ALPR providing labels), but the volume is not yet sufficient for model training. The on-device model improvement is a future sprint gated by data availability, not by architectural feasibility.

Vehicle-Only Classification on Device

The background scan path transmits only plate text during the initial scan, before any watchlist match is known. It does not perform vehicle classification (color, body type, make/model) because YOLO runs server-side, and the only frame the path ever uploads is the single evidence frame that follows a confirmed plate match — too late to help match a profile-only alert (one that describes a vehicle but provides no plate number). A future enhancement could run a lightweight vehicle classification model on-device alongside ML Kit, transmitting vehicle attributes as additional form fields without transmitting any image data during the scan itself. This would enable profile-only matching from the background scan path, expanding coverage while keeping the same evidence-frame-only-on-a-hit guarantee.

Evidence Frame on Watchlist Hit — Built June 28, 2026

This section originally described a hypothetical: a future, consent-gated frame upload for high-confidence matches, guarded by an explicit “would you like to upload a photo?” prompt. That is not what got built. What shipped instead follows the same evidentiary standard as the drone path: on a confirmed watchlist hit, the scan service automatically uploads the one frame that produced the match, with no per-detection prompt. The disclosure lives in the Privacy Policy and Terms of Service, which volunteers accept before scanning — not in a live consent dialog per hit.

The architectural guarantee this paper is actually built around is narrower than “the scan service never uploads frames autonomously,” and more precise: the scan service never uploads frames for non-matching scans. That boundary is still enforced in native code, still requires a new compiled build and app store review to move, and is the guarantee volunteers should be told about. Extending frame capture any further — to non-hit frames, or to any other passive/background scanning path — is treated as a hard line and would require exactly the kind of consent flow this section originally proposed.

Conclusion

The distinction between policy-based and architecture-based privacy is not academic. It is the difference between a promise and a constraint. Promises are broken by board votes, acquisition agreements, and quarterly revenue targets. Constraints are broken by code changes that go through version control, code review, native compilation, and app store submission.

Amber’s Angels chose the constraint. On both Android and iOS, the phone scanning path runs on-device OCR, transmits text, and discards the frame for every non-matching scan. No configuration toggle or server-side flag enables frame upload beyond that; getting from “discard” to “upload one evidence frame on a hit” took exactly what this paper predicted — new native code, compiled, submitted to Google and Apple, and reviewed — producing an auditable, public, reviewable change in the process, not a silent policy update.

When a volunteer asks “is my phone recording my neighbors?”, I do not answer with a privacy policy. I answer with an architecture. The phone reads plates and sends text; the frame is discarded unless that plate is the one we're looking for, in which case one frame is saved as evidence, the same as it would be from a drone. That is how the code works, and code does not lie.
