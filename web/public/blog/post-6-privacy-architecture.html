Privacy by Architecture,
Not Policy

By Grant Lindberg, Founder & Executive Director — Amber's Angels Inc.  ·  July 21, 2026

There's a meaningful difference between a privacy policy and a privacy architecture. A policy says what you're allowed to do with data. A constraint makes certain uses architecturally impossible. I spent years in a job where understanding that difference was the work itself.

What the Fourth Amendment Auditor Learned

As a signals intelligence professional in the U.S. Army, I was part of routine audits to ensure collection activities complied with Fourth Amendment standards. My unit operated with aerial intelligence collection capabilities in Korea, and part of the operational framework was systematic review of what was collected, how it was collected, and whether it was constitutionally permissible.

That work wasn't abstract legal theory. It was applied judgment about what a system could lawfully do — and what constraints needed to exist to ensure it stayed within those limits.

The most important thing I took from that role: the most reliable constraint isn't a policy that says "we won't do X." It's an architecture that makes X impossible.

"A policy can be changed. A policy can be overridden by legal compulsion. A policy can be circumvented. An architectural constraint cannot. The data that doesn't exist cannot be subpoenaed."

What Amber's Angels Actually Retains

Let me be precise, because I've seen vague privacy claims in this space and I don't want to make one.

Phone scanning (Android and iOS) — the default volunteer experience

License plate recognition runs entirely on the volunteer's phone — ML Kit on Android, Vision framework on iOS. For every detection, only plate text, a confidence score, and GPS coordinates are transmitted to the server. Camera frames stay on the device unless the server confirms a watchlist hit. On a confirmed hit, one frame is uploaded as evidence — because if we found the car, that frame matters. Non-matching frames are never transmitted. This is an architectural constraint, not a policy.

Drone frames — innocent (non-matching) frames

Drone video feeds are processed server-side. Non-matching frames are held in RAM for the duration of AI inference — typically milliseconds — then released. No write path exists for innocent frames. You cannot retrieve them because they were never stored.

High-confidence match frames — the frames that matter

Shared with law enforcement coordinators as part of the active case. Retained as detection records for one year for law enforcement case reference. A match frame is evidence. It has a name attached to it: the child we're trying to find. We keep it. We should keep it. This is the point of the system.

Volunteer GPS telemetry

Transmitted during active missions for coordinator situational awareness. Retained for 90 days for mission analysis. Deleted on schedule via automated routine.

What we do not retain

No database of vehicle movements. No record of innocent vehicles observed. No footage archive. No data available to market, share, or repurpose. No persistent record of where anyone was during a search session — only whether their vehicle matched an active alert profile.

Why This Matters for Law Enforcement Partners

Law enforcement agencies that partner with Amber's Angels don't inherit our data liability — because we don't have data liability to inherit. There is no database of innocent vehicle movements that can be subpoenaed in a civil case, disclosed in an investigation, or accessed by a bad actor.

The data that doesn't exist cannot create problems. That's the whole point of the architecture.

For agencies already navigating the community trust challenges that come with always-on surveillance infrastructure, a partner platform that produces no persistent data about innocent people is a different kind of conversation with their communities.

The Honest Limitation

No-retention architecture means no reach-back capability. I've said this in previous posts and I'll say it again here: we cannot tell you what drove past a volunteer last Tuesday. We cannot answer investigative queries about historical vehicle movements. That capability requires retention.

We made a trade. For missing persons response — where the question is what's happening right now — we gave up retrospective capability to eliminate retention liability. That trade makes sense for our specific use case. It won't make sense for every use case.

The architecture should match the problem. For the problem we're solving, this is the right architecture.
