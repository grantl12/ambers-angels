BOLO ingestor: pulling alerts from outside the federal feed

Not all actionable vehicle data arrives via FEMA IPAWS. Local law enforcement routinely posts BOLOs on departmental Facebook pages, county sheriff websites, and state agency news feeds, often before a formal WEA alert is issued. The BOLO ingestor is our attempt to bridge that gap.

Automated monitoring

A background process checks about 10 sources every 15 minutes: FBI kidnapping and missing persons feeds, NCMEC press releases, GBI Georgia, ALEA Alabama, TBI Tennessee, and others. Coordinators can add county sheriff feeds from the admin panel. Extraction runs entirely on our servers. Only posts with enough vehicle data to be actionable — a plate, or a make + model, or at minimum a make + color + area — create an alert.

Screenshot upload

When a coordinator spots a BOLO the automated process missed — a Facebook post, a regional alert, a screenshot forwarded by a volunteer — they can upload it directly. The flow:

1
Upload. Coordinator selects a screenshot in the admin panel and submits it.
2
AI reads the image. A vision AI model scans the screenshot and extracts: plate number, person name, vehicle color, make and model, location, and what type of alert it represents.
3
Confirmation gate. A preview card shows the coordinator exactly what will go live. Nothing is activated yet. They can discard and re-upload a cleaner image if the extraction looks wrong.
4
Activation. Coordinator taps "Alert the Angels." The plate goes onto the active watchlist, volunteers in the area receive push notifications, and a Discord alert fires with a reference photo of the vehicle when one is available.
5
Permanent record. Every ingested alert is written to a log table that is never purged — source, extracted vehicle description, number of volunteers notified, whether a Discord alert fired. Operational alert records should not share a retention policy with user-collected scan data.
ℹ
The confirmation gate is deliberate. The AI occasionally misreads a plate character or miscategorizes an alert type on a blurry image. A human has to approve every BOLO ingest before it reaches volunteers, regardless of source.
02

Reading CAP alerts: before and after abbreviation handling

FEMA IPAWS CMAS alerts are constrained by WEA's 90-character message limit. The underlying CAP data that dispatching agencies transmit uses heavy abbreviation — the same shorthand broadcasters and dispatchers have used for decades over radio. Our system was initially reading those abbreviations as literal text and finding nothing.

What the raw CAP text actually looks like

This is a representative example in authentic TBI dispatch style:

AMBER ALERT - TN 05/15/26
SUSP VEH: 17 BLK CHEV SLVRD PU LP TN/CLZ4471
KAYLEE R TANNER WF 9YOA BLN HAIR BLU EYS
SUSP: DEREK J TANNER WM 38YOA ARMED AND DANGEROUS
LKA: NASHVILLE TN / I-40 WESTBOUND
Before — reading abbreviations literally
plateCLZ4471
colornot found
body typenot found
makenot found
modelnot found
yearnot found
After — abbreviation expansion
plateCLZ4471
colorblack
body typepickup
makeChevrolet
modelSilverado
year2017

How the expansion works

Before the system tries to read vehicle data, it runs the raw text through a translation table of about 100 abbreviations: color codes (BLK→black, SLV→silver, WHT→white), makes (CHEV→chevrolet, TOYT→toyota), body types (PU→pickup, TRK→truck), and model shorthand (SLVRD→silverado, ALTMA→altima). Once the text reads like English, standard pattern matching works.

Year extraction had a subtle bug: the system was reading 05 from the date at the top of the alert (05/15/26) and returning 2005 instead of 2017. It now ignores numbers that appear next to date separators, so only the year in the vehicle line counts.

Why recovering the full vehicle profile matters

A plate number is necessary but not sufficient for building volunteer confidence during a search. The full vehicle profile enables three things that can't happen with a bare plate:

Capability Needs What it does
Visual vehicle matching color or body type The drone or phone camera can confirm or reject a candidate vehicle by color and shape before the plate is even readable. A mismatch subtracts from the confidence score; a match adds.
Statistical confidence adjustment body type Minivans are historically over-represented in child abduction cases vs. the national vehicle fleet. The system weights body type matches accordingly.
Reference photo for volunteers make + model A stock photo of a matching vehicle attaches to the Discord alert and push notification so volunteers know what they're actually looking for.
03

The timing gap problem

The interval between when an event occurs and when volunteer searchers have the information they need is the problem I think about most. It's the most important operational variable and the one I have the least leverage over.

The formal alert path has built-in latency: officers gather enough information to assess validity and construct a complete vehicle description, that description goes through the IPAWS authoring system, FEMA broadcasts to carriers, carriers push WEA to handsets. That validation window is non-negotiable from a false-alert perspective, but it's real time off the clock. We are completely dependent on what scraps of information those agencies decide to share publicly — we have no integration with their feeds or dispatch systems.

The case for allowing verified law enforcement coordinators to push alerts directly is specifically to compress that window. A dispatcher could notify our volunteers while the formal IPAWS alert is still being composed. Right now I rely on departmental email domains as a soft verification step and keep a full audit log of every activation, but I don't have a reliable way to confirm credentials programmatically. That's an open limitation and an open design question I'd genuinely welcome input on — what criteria should be built in so the capability can't be misused?

⚠
Direct law enforcement activation is a live design question. The tradeoff is speed vs. validation: faster notification, but with less formal gatekeeping than the IPAWS path. An MOU and formal criteria for issuance would need to come before we market this to agencies. We'd want to build in the same kind of minimum criteria the AMBER system uses, or something comparable, so it isn't activated for every minor incident.
04

Alert fatigue, privacy, and opt-in design

The standard concern with expanded alert reach is fatigue. Too many alerts erodes compliance with all of them. I think opt-in volunteer design genuinely changes that calculus compared to WEA broadcast, for a few reasons.

Volunteers chose to register and set their own geographic alert range. They can narrow that range if they're getting more than they want to act on. The "every teen who missed curfew" problem doesn't land the same way when someone is choosing to receive the alert and showing up with that behavioral commitment. Alert fatigue is still real but the population dynamics are different.

On location accuracy: older handsets report coarse location, and WEA radius targeting can mis-locate them by several miles. Volunteers in our system transmit phone GPS approximately once per second during active searches, so we know where they actually are. That lets us be more targeted with who gets alerted and reduces spurious notifications for people outside the actual search area.

On privacy: the platform has to be deliberately activated. There is no passive background collection. On Android, a persistent notification shows that scanning is running and has a one-tap stop button. On iOS, the camera screen must stay open. Neither runs without explicit user action.

One feature I built and then disabled illustrates the tension well. If a volunteer is driving through several counties on a road trip, the idea was to automatically load BOLOs for each jurisdiction they entered and scan continuously throughout. That worked. I shut it off because a bad actor could use stagnant regional BOLOs as justification to drive several counties while running a plate reader on everyone they passed. That's not what this is for.

Structural minimum search criteria

At its core this is a license plate reader platform. Searches without a plate, or at minimum a make and color and body type, don't produce results the system can act on. That's an implicit minimum criteria threshold — not a policy decision, just the reality of what optical character recognition can do. The one exception is below.

Purple Alert: the one search without a vehicle

Purple Alert (developmental disability)

Missing adults with developmental disabilities frequently seek water: ponds, creeks, drainage ditches, retention basins. Several states issue Purple Alerts for this population via FEMA IPAWS CMAS. It's the only alert type where vehicle data isn't the primary search lead.

When we receive a Purple Alert, all notifications carry an extra line: "Persons with developmental disabilities often seek water. Check ponds, creeks, drainage ditches, and retention basins first."

The more interesting piece: coordinators receive an automatically generated list of bodies of water near the incident location, pulled from public map data. From that list they can:

  • Assign specific water bodies to available ground volunteers by map pin
  • Dispatch drones automatically to hover over uncovered locations
  • Track coverage: which sites have been checked, which are still open

The drone dispatch for water coverage uses the same autonomous flight path as standard observation missions. The Purple Alert context just pre-populates the mission targets from the water list rather than requiring a coordinator to manually drop pins.

✓
Purple Alert is the clearest example of alert type shaping search behavior rather than just notification. The vehicle pipeline is bypassed entirely. The operational logic changes because the missing person profile changes.
