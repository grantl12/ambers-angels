01

BOLO Ingester — end-to-end flow

Not all actionable vehicle data arrives via FEMA IPAWS. Local law enforcement routinely posts BOLOs on departmental Facebook pages, county sheriff websites, and state agency news feeds — often before a formal WEA alert is issued. The BOLO ingestor bridges that gap.

Automated crawler path

A configurable crawler polls ~10 sources every 15 minutes: FBI kidnapping/missing RSS, NCMEC press releases, GBI Georgia, ALEA Alabama, TBI Tennessee, and others. Coordinators can add county sheriff feeds from the admin panel. Extraction uses regex, dictionary matching, and spaCy NER — no API call per post. Only posts with sufficient vehicle data (a plate, or make + model, or make + color + area) create an alert.

Screenshot upload path

When a coordinator spots a BOLO that the crawler missed — a Facebook post, a regional NextDoor alert, a screenshot forwarded by a volunteer — they upload it directly. The flow:

1
Upload. Coordinator picks the screenshot in the admin panel. The image (JPEG/PNG) is POSTed to /admin/preview-bolo.
2
AI extraction. Claude Haiku (vision) receives the image with a structured prompt asking for: plate, person name, vehicle color, vehicle make/model, area, and alert classification. Returns structured JSON.
3
Confirmation gate. A preview card shows the coordinator exactly what will be written to the watchlist. Nothing is activated yet. The coordinator can discard and re-upload a cleaner image.
4
Activation. Coordinator taps "Alert the Angels." Watchlist entry created, vehicle target written, Discord embed fires (with Wikimedia vehicle photo if available), push notifications sent to pilots in the geographic area.
5
Logged permanently. Every ingested alert — source, extracted vehicle profile, raw text, pilots notified, whether Discord fired — is written to alert_ingestion_log, a permanent table with no purge TTL. (Data governance note below.)
ℹ
The confirmation gate is intentional. Haiku occasionally misreads a plate character or miscategorizes an alert type on a blurry image. Requiring a human confirmation step before watchlist activation keeps a coordinator in the loop on every BOLO ingest, regardless of source.
02

FEMA CAP parser — before & after jargon normalization

FEMA IPAWS CMAS alerts are constrained by WEA's 90-character message limit. The CAP XML that dispatching agencies transmit uses heavy abbreviation — the same shorthand broadcasters and dispatchers have used for decades over radio. Our initial parser matched full English words and missed almost everything.

Representative input (authentic TBI dispatch style)

AMBER ALERT - TN 05/15/26
SUSP VEH: 17 BLK CHEV SLVRD PU LP TN/CLZ4471
KAYLEE R TANNER WF 9YOA BLN HAIR BLU EYS
SUSP: DEREK J TANNER WM 38YOA ARMED AND DANGEROUS
LKA: NASHVILLE TN / I-40 WESTBOUND
Before — no normalization
plateCLZ4471
colorNone
body typeNone
makeNone
modelNone
yearNone
After — WEA normalization pass
plateCLZ4471
colorblack
body typepickup
makeChevrolet
modelSilverado
year2017

What the normalization pass does

Before any regex matching, the raw CAP text goes through ~50 substitution patterns: color codes (BLK→black, SLV→silver, WHT→white), makes (CHEV/CHVY→chevrolet, TOYT→toyota, HOMD→honda), body types (PU/P-U→pickup, TRK→truck, SED→sedan), and ~50 model name abbreviations (SLVRD→silverado, ACCORD→accord, ALTMA→altima).

Year extraction required a secondary fix. The pattern was initially matching 05 from the date string 05/15/26 in the headline and returning 2005. A negative lookahead excludes 2-digit tokens followed by /, so the correct 17 in 17 BLK CHEV wins.

Why this matters downstream

The vehicle profile unlocks three things that don't fire without it:

Feature Requires Effect
YOLO corroboration color or body type +14 pts composite confidence on match; −12/−8 pts on mismatch
Bayesian prior bonus body type +7 pts for minivan in AMBER (historically over-represented); adjusts score against national fleet distribution
Vehicle reference photo make + model Wikimedia Commons stock photo attached to Discord embed and push card so volunteers know what they're looking for
03

The timing gap problem

The interval between when a triggering event occurs and when widespread notification reaches potential searchers is the problem I spend the most time on. It's the most important operational metric and the one I have the least leverage over.

The formal alert path has built-in latency: the event occurs, LEOs gather enough information to assess validity and construct a complete vehicle/plate description, that description goes through the IPAWS authoring system, FEMA broadcasts to carriers, carriers push WEA to handsets. That validation window — the time when LEOs are deciding whether the situation meets criteria — is non-negotiable from a false-alert perspective but it's real time off the clock.

The logic behind LEA-activated searches (coordinators with verified departmental email addresses being able to create alerts directly) is specifically to compress that window for agencies that are willing to engage with the platform. A dispatcher could push a BOLO to our volunteers while the formal IPAWS alert is still being composed. I don't have a programmatic way to verify LEA credentials yet — I rely on departmental email domains and keep the activation in an audit log — but the capability is there.

⚠
Direct LEA activation is an open design question. The tradeoff is speed vs. validation: a dispatcher-created alert could go to volunteers faster but with less validation overhead than the IPAWS path. I'd want an MOU and formal vetting process before marketing this capability to agencies.
04

Alert fatigue, privacy, and opt-in architecture

The standard concern with expanded alert reach is fatigue — too many alerts erodes compliance with all of them. I think opt-in volunteer architecture genuinely changes that calculus compared to WEA broadcast.

Our volunteers registered and were vetted. They set their geographic alert range. They can narrow that range if they're getting more alerts than they want to act on. The "every teen who missed curfew" problem doesn't apply the same way when the person receiving the alert is choosing to receive it and has established behavioral commitment through registration. Alert fatigue is still a real concern but the population dynamics are different than broadcast.

On privacy: the platform has to be deliberately activated. There is no passive background collection. On Android, volunteers run a foreground service with a persistent notification showing an active frame count and a one-tap stop — the system makes it visible that scanning is happening. On iOS, the camera screen must stay open. Neither runs without explicit user action.

One feature I built and then disabled illustrates the tension: dynamic area-based BOLO loading. The idea was that if a volunteer is driving a road trip through multiple counties, the app would automatically load BOLOs for whatever jurisdiction they were driving through and scan continuously. Technically that worked. I disabled it for privacy reasons — a bad actor could use stagnant regional BOLOs as cover to drive several counties while actively scanning every plate they pass. That's not what the platform is for.

◆
GPS-based alert scoping also improves on WEA architecture in one concrete way: older handsets report coarse location, and WEA radius targeting can mis-locate them by several miles. Our volunteers transmit phone GPS at ~1 Hz during active missions — we know where they actually are.

Minimum search criteria

At core this is a license plate reader platform. Searches without a plate or at minimum a make + color + body type don't produce actionable ALPR results, so the system is structurally biased toward vehicle searches. That's an implicit minimum criteria threshold — not a policy decision, just the reality of what ALPR can do. The one exception is below.

Purple Alert — the one search without a vehicle

Purple Alert (developmental disability)

Missing adults with developmental disabilities frequently seek water — ponds, creeks, drainage ditches, retention basins. Several states issue Purple Alerts for this population via FEMA IPAWS CMAS. Purple is the only alert type where vehicle data isn't the primary search lead.

When we ingest a Purple Alert, all notifications carry an extra advisory: "Persons with developmental disabilities often seek water. Check ponds, creeks, drainage ditches, and retention basins first."

The more interesting capability: coordinators receive an AI-generated list of bodies of water near the incident report location, pulled from OpenStreetMap via the Overpass API. From that list they can:

  • Assign specific water bodies to available ground volunteers by map pin
  • Dispatch drones automatically to hover over uncovered water bodies
  • Track coverage — which locations have been checked, which are still open

The drone dispatch for water coverage is the same MSDK V5 autonomous waypoint path as standard observation missions. The Purple Alert context just pre-populates the mission targets from the water body list rather than requiring a coordinator to manually drop observation pins.

✓
Purple Alert is the clearest example in the platform of alert type driving search behavior rather than just notification. The vehicle pipeline is bypassed entirely; the operational logic changes because the missing person profile changes.
